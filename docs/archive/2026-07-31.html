🧠 Data Science Topic of the Day

🎓 A/B Testing & Experimentation at Scale

Replacing gut feel with ground truth — how to know if a change actually works.

What It Is

A/B testing is a controlled experiment that splits a population into two (or more) groups, exposes each group to a different treatment (a model, a message, a price, a feature), and measures whether the observed difference in outcomes is real or just random noise. In a business context it is the gold standard for causal claims about product or model changes — not 'we think this works' but 'we proved it works at 95% confidence.' At scale, A/B testing extends into multi-armed bandits (which dynamically reallocate traffic to winning variants), factorial designs (testing multiple variables simultaneously), and switchback experiments (for settings where user-level randomisation is impossible, like pricing or marketplace liquidity).

How It Works

1. DEFINE THE ESTIMAND: State the metric that matters (conversion rate, 7-day retention, average revenue per account) and the minimum detectable effect (MDE) — the smallest lift that would be commercially meaningful. The MDE drives sample size via a power calculation (typically 80% power, α=0.05). Under-powered tests produce false negatives; skipping this step is the single most common experimentation mistake. 2. RANDOMISE CORRECTLY: Assign units (users, accounts, DUNS numbers) to control/treatment using a hash of a stable ID so assignment is deterministic and reproducible. Cluster randomisation (by company or territory) is required when treatment spillover is likely — e.g., if a sales rep sees both control and treatment accounts, contamination invalidates the test. 3. RUN THE TEST: Respect the pre-specified runtime. Peeking early and stopping when p<0.05 inflates Type I error dramatically — this is optional stopping bias. Use sequential testing methods (e.g., always-valid p-values, mSPRT) if you genuinely need to monitor continuously. 4. ANALYSE: For binary outcomes (converted / not), use a two-proportion z-test or a chi-squared test. For continuous outcomes (revenue, score), use Welch's t-test or a Mann-Whitney U if the distribution is heavy-tailed. Report confidence intervals, not just p-values — the interval tells you the range of plausible effects, which matters for ROI decisions. 5. GUARD AGAINST NOVELTY EFFECTS & NETWORK EFFECTS: A new UI always gets a short-term bump. Run long enough to see steady-state behaviour. If units interact (a buyer and a supplier on the same platform), use graph-cluster randomisation or a holdout design. 6. SEGMENT & HETEROGENEOUS TREATMENT EFFECTS (HTE): Aggregate lift can mask subgroup wins and losses. Use CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) to reduce variance, and tree-based HTE methods (causal forests) to find which segments respond best — this is where experimentation connects back to targeting strategy.

When To Use

— Validating a new predictive model before full rollout (champion vs. challenger) — Testing pricing, packaging, or offer messaging — Evaluating a new data signal or feature (does adding Paydex to a propensity model actually lift pipeline conversion?) — Measuring the causal impact of a sales play or marketing campaign — Comparing two entity resolution strategies on match precision/recall in downstream revenue outcomes Do NOT use A/B testing when: you have too few units (< ~200 per arm for most metrics), the outcome takes years to materialise (use proxy metrics carefully), or the ethical/legal framework prohibits differential treatment.

🎯 Worked Example — D&B Context

SCENARIO — Does Buydex-enriched outreach lift enterprise pipeline conversion? D&B's Prospecting Agent surfaces target accounts and drafts outreach. The team hypothesises that pre-populating the outreach with the account's Buydex buying-intent signal ('this company is actively researching ERP vendors') will lift booked-meeting rate versus generic outreach. STEP 1 — POWER CALC: Historical booked-meeting rate = 4%. The team wants to detect a 1 pp lift (to 5%) — a 25% relative increase. At 80% power, α=0.05, two-sided: n ≈ 2,900 accounts per arm. With ~500 outreach sequences per week, runtime = ~6 weeks. STEP 2 — RANDOMISE: Hash each DUNS number to control (standard outreach) or treatment (Buydex-enriched outreach). Cluster by sales rep territory to prevent rep-level contamination — a rep should not work both arms simultaneously. STEP 3 — RUN: 6 weeks. No peeking. Outcome = 'meeting booked within 14 days of outreach.' STEP 4 — ANALYSE: Control: 118/2,940 = 4.01%. Treatment: 156/2,955 = 5.28%. Z-test: z = 3.1, p = 0.002, 95% CI on lift = [0.47pp, 1.77pp]. Statistically and commercially significant. STEP 5 — HTE: Causal forest reveals the lift is concentrated in mid-market accounts (100–999 employees) with Buydex intent score > 70. Enterprise (1000+) shows no incremental lift — likely because enterprise reps already have deep account knowledge that the signal does not add to. DECISION: Roll out Buydex enrichment to mid-market sequences immediately. Run a follow-on test for enterprise with a different signal (e.g., corporate family tree expansion triggers). The experiment has also produced a clean, auditable, explainable result — exactly the 'Ground Truth' posture D&B should be able to demonstrate to customers evaluating the ROI of D&B data in their own agentic workflows.
