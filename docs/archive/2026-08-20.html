🧠 Data Science Topic of the Day

🎓 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

How AI models learn to be useful by treating human preferences as the reward signal

What It Is

RLHF is the training technique behind most modern large language models — GPT-4, Claude, Gemini. It takes a base model that can predict text and transforms it into one that follows instructions, stays on-topic, and avoids harmful outputs. The key insight is that 'correctness' for language tasks is hard to define mathematically, but humans can reliably say 'this answer is better than that one.' RLHF turns those pairwise human judgments into a trainable reward signal. It is why the same underlying architecture that hallucinates freely as a raw model becomes a helpful assistant after fine-tuning. For D&B, it is the mechanism that determines whether an AI agent embedded in Salesforce Agentforce or Microsoft Copilot actually behaves like a trusted business intelligence layer — or like an overconfident autocomplete engine.

How It Works

RLHF runs in three sequential stages. Stage 1 — Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): human annotators write ideal responses to a sample of prompts; the base model is fine-tuned on these demonstrations to produce a reasonable starting point. Stage 2 — Reward Model Training: the SFT model generates multiple responses to the same prompt; human raters rank them from best to worst; a separate neural network (the Reward Model, RM) is trained to predict those rankings, effectively learning a scalar 'quality score' for any (prompt, response) pair. Stage 3 — RL Optimisation via PPO: the SFT model is now treated as a policy in a reinforcement learning loop. It generates responses; the Reward Model scores them; Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) updates the policy's weights to produce higher-scoring outputs. A KL-divergence penalty prevents the model from drifting too far from the original SFT checkpoint — without it, the policy 'reward hacks' by producing outputs the RM scores highly but that are nonsensical to real humans. A critical modern variant is Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO), which skips the explicit RL loop entirely and optimises preferences directly via a re-weighted classification loss — simpler, more stable, and increasingly preferred in production. The whole system is only as good as the annotators: their consistency, their domain expertise, and the diversity of prompts they evaluate all propagate directly into model behaviour.

When To Use

Use RLHF (or DPO) when: (1) you are fine-tuning a foundation model for a specific enterprise persona — e.g., a D&B 'Account Intelligence Agent' that must cite sources, hedge uncertain predictions, and never invent DUNS numbers; (2) your quality criteria are subjective or multi-dimensional and cannot be captured by a single loss function — accuracy, tone, brevity, compliance language all matter simultaneously; (3) you have access to domain experts who can rank outputs, even in small numbers (a few thousand preference pairs can meaningfully shift model behaviour); (4) you need to suppress specific failure modes — hallucinated company financials, invented firmographic attributes, or confident wrong answers on entity resolution. Avoid it when you have fewer than ~1,000 high-quality preference pairs, when a simpler instruction-tuned model already meets your bar, or when annotation consistency across raters cannot be controlled — noisy human labels produce a noisy Reward Model and the RL loop amplifies that noise.

🎯 Worked Example — D&B Context

D&B is embedding a Prospecting Agent into Salesforce Agentforce. The agent takes a rep's Ideal Customer Profile and returns a ranked list of net-new prospects with supporting rationale — industry fit, Paydex score trajectory, corporate family tree context, recent intent signals from Bombora. The base LLM, straight off the shelf, sometimes invents DUNS numbers, overstates confidence on sparse records, and writes rationale in dense analyst prose that field reps ignore. The RLHF fix: (1) SFT — D&B's sales analytics team writes 500 gold-standard prospect recommendations using real D&B data, modelling the exact format, hedging language, and source-citation style required. (2) Reward Model — the agent generates five alternative recommendations for 2,000 real rep queries; experienced D&B sales engineers rank them, penalising hallucinated attributes, rewarding clear sourcing ('Paydex 12-month trend: 74→81, as of Q2 2026') and appropriate confidence hedging on thin-file SMBs. (3) PPO fine-tuning — the policy learns that citing a DUNS-verified attribute scores higher than an inferred one, that 'data not available' beats a fabricated revenue figure, and that three-sentence rationale outperforms twelve. Post-RLHF, hallucination on firmographic attributes drops measurably, rep adoption increases because the tone matches how they actually talk about prospects, and the agent self-flags when a record has fewer than three D&B data touches — precisely the explainability-first behaviour D&B's brand promise requires. The Reward Model itself becomes a reusable asset: it can evaluate any new agent output before it reaches the rep, functioning as an automated quality gate in the MLOps pipeline.
