🧠 Data Science Topic of the Day

🎓 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

How you teach a model to do what you actually mean, not just what you said.

What It Is

RLHF is the training technique that bridges the gap between a model that predicts plausible text and one that behaves usefully and safely. It works by incorporating human judgments — preferences, rankings, corrections — directly into the optimization loop. This is the core method behind the alignment of GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and Llama variants. For D&B, it is the mechanism that would let you take a capable but generic LLM and shape it into a specialist that reliably produces explainable, commercially grounded outputs rather than confident-sounding hallucinations about firmographic data.

How It Works

RLHF runs in three sequential stages. Stage 1 — Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): You collect a set of high-quality prompt-response pairs written or curated by domain experts and fine-tune the base model on them. This anchors the model to your domain — think of it as teaching it the vocabulary and norms of B2B commercial intelligence. Stage 2 — Reward Model Training: You take pairs of model outputs for the same prompt and ask human raters (or expert annotators) to say which response is better and why. A separate neural network — the Reward Model (RM) — is trained to predict these preference scores. It learns a continuous signal for 'quality' from discrete human judgments. Stage 3 — RL Fine-Tuning via PPO: The main LLM is now treated as an RL policy. It generates responses, the Reward Model scores them, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) — a stable, clipping-based policy gradient algorithm — nudges the LLM's weights toward responses that score higher. A KL-divergence penalty is applied to stop the model drifting too far from the SFT baseline and collapsing into reward hacking. The result is a model whose outputs consistently reflect the preferences encoded by your annotators. A newer, simpler alternative — Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) — skips the explicit RM and PPO loop entirely, directly optimizing the LLM on preference pairs using a reparameterized loss. DPO is cheaper and more stable, and is increasingly preferred for domain-specific fine-tuning.

When To Use

Use RLHF (or DPO as its practical successor) when: (1) Output quality is subjective or nuanced — standard loss functions cannot capture 'this account summary is commercially trustworthy' vs 'this one sounds confident but is wrong.' (2) You have domain experts who can rank outputs but cannot easily write training data from scratch. (3) You need behavioral guardrails — for example, ensuring an AI agent never invents a DUNS number, never fabricates a Paydex score, and always cites its data lineage. (4) A model is capable but miscalibrated — it knows enough about B2B entities but consistently over-hedges or under-specifies. Avoid RLHF when your annotation budget is tiny (fewer than ~1,000 preference pairs), when the task has a clear objective metric (use standard fine-tuning), or when DPO can achieve the same alignment goal at a fraction of the compute cost.

🎯 Worked Example — D&B Context

D&B's Prospecting Agent generates natural-language company summaries to help sales reps prioritize outbound targets. After initial SFT on curated D&B analyst write-ups, the model produces plausible summaries — but reps complain that it sometimes inflates growth signals, buries credit risk flags, and fails to mention subsidiary relationships that are commercially critical. You run an RLHF alignment cycle: (1) SFT baseline: fine-tune on 2,000 verified analyst summaries tagged with entity-level data from the D&B Data Cloud. (2) Preference data collection: for 800 prompt pairs, two senior commercial analysts rank which of two model-generated summaries they would trust to brief an enterprise AE — annotating why (e.g., 'correctly surfaced parent entity,' 'Paydex trend cited accurately,' 'did not confabulate revenue figure'). (3) Reward Model training: a lightweight RM (e.g., a DeBERTa classifier) learns to predict analyst preference. It discovers that correctly invoking corporate family tree structure and explicitly flagging data freshness date are the strongest predictors of a high-quality rating. (4) PPO loop: the Prospecting Agent LLM is fine-tuned against the RM for 3 epochs with a KL penalty of 0.02 — keeping outputs grounded. Post-RLHF, rep trust scores (measured in downstream pipeline progression rates) rise 18%, and hallucinated financial figures drop by 74% on a held-out evaluation set. The same RM can now be reused to score and filter outputs from the Account Expansion and Pipeline Risk agents — a flywheel effect where one annotation investment improves alignment across the entire six-agent suite.
