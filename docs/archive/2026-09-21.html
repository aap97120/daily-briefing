🧠 Data Science Topic of the Day

🎓 Causal Inference

Moving beyond 'what correlates' to 'what actually causes' — the difference between a dashboard and a decision.

What It Is

Causal inference is a framework for estimating the true effect of an intervention (a treatment, a policy, a product change) on an outcome, holding everything else constant. Where standard ML finds patterns that predict, causal inference answers counterfactual questions: 'What would have happened to this customer's revenue if we had NOT assigned them a dedicated account manager?' It draws on econometrics, statistics, and graph theory — tools include Potential Outcomes (Rubin Causal Model), Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs), and estimators like Difference-in-Differences (DiD), Instrumental Variables (IV), and Regression Discontinuity (RD). The goal is to isolate signal from confounding noise so that business decisions are grounded in cause, not coincidence.

How It Works

1. DEFINE THE CAUSAL QUESTION: State explicitly what treatment T you are studying and what outcome Y you care about. Example: Does receiving a D&B Credibility Review badge (T) increase a business's inbound leads (Y)? 2. DRAW A DAG: Map all variables — confounders (C) that affect both T and Y, mediators (M) on the causal path, and colliders to avoid conditioning on. This forces explicit assumptions before touching data. 3. IDENTIFY THE ESTIMAND: Choose your target quantity — Average Treatment Effect (ATE), Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT), or a Conditional ATE (CATE) for heterogeneous effects. 4. CHOOSE AN ESTIMATOR: — Randomised experiment (RCT): gold standard; randomly assign treatment. — Propensity Score Matching / IPW: when RCT is impossible, model P(T=1|C) and reweight or match treated vs. control units to create a pseudo-randomised comparison group. — Difference-in-Differences (DiD): compare the change in Y before/after treatment for treated units vs. a control group that didn't receive treatment — cancels out time-invariant confounders. — Regression Discontinuity (RD): exploit a sharp threshold rule (e.g., firms with a Paydex score ≥ 80 automatically qualify for a credit product) to compare firms just above vs. just below the cutoff. — Instrumental Variables (IV): find a variable Z that affects T but affects Y only through T — isolates exogenous variation in treatment. 5. CHECK ASSUMPTIONS: Each estimator has untestable core assumptions (e.g., parallel trends for DiD, no unmeasured confounding for IPW). Sensitivity analyses and falsification tests (placebo outcomes, pre-trend tests) stress-test them. 6. ESTIMATE & INTERPRET: Fit the model, compute the causal effect with confidence intervals, and translate back to business language — not 'coefficient = 0.12' but 'receiving the badge causes a 12-percentage-point lift in inbound leads on average.'

When To Use

Use causal inference whenever a stakeholder asks 'Did X work?' or 'What happens if we do Y?': — Measuring ROI of sales interventions (did adding a D&B intent signal to the CRM actually shorten deal cycles?). — Evaluating pricing policy changes without a clean A/B test. — Attributing revenue to marketing touchpoints beyond last-click. — Estimating the effect of a Paydex score drop on a firm's future delinquency rate — controlling for industry and macro conditions. — Any situation where pure prediction models have been weaponised ('our churn model is 91% accurate') but no one has asked whether the intervention driven by that model actually reduced churn. Causal inference is NOT needed when the goal is purely predictive (ranking leads, scoring risk) and no action-effect question is being asked.

🎯 Worked Example — D&B Context

SCENARIO — Does D&B Finance Analytics' early-warning alert (treatment T) reduce accounts receivable days outstanding (DSO) for commercial banking clients? PROBLEM: D&B's product team wants to prove the AR alert feature reduces DSO. A naive comparison shows alert users average 38 DSO vs. 47 DSO for non-users — but alert users are self-selected: they are the most sophisticated, data-driven treasury teams who would likely have lower DSO anyway (confounding). APPROACH — Difference-in-Differences: — PRE/POST: Measure DSO for both groups 6 months before and 6 months after the alert feature was launched (a known date = a clean intervention point). — TREATED GROUP: Clients who activated the alert within the first 30 days of launch. — CONTROL GROUP: Clients who had access but did not activate (matched on firm size, industry, baseline DSO, and Paydex score using propensity score matching to improve comparability). — PARALLEL TRENDS TEST: Verify that treated and control groups had statistically indistinguishable DSO trends in the 12 months prior to launch — if trends diverged pre-launch, DiD assumptions fail. — ESTIMATE: DiD coefficient = (DSO_treated_after − DSO_treated_before) − (DSO_control_after − DSO_control_before). RESULT: After controlling for confounders via matching + DiD, the causal estimate is −4.2 DSO days (95% CI: −6.1 to −2.3 days), statistically significant. The naive 9-day gap shrinks to 4.2 days — still commercially meaningful (at $500M AR, 4.2 days = ~$5.75M in freed working capital) but far more credible and defensible in a CFO conversation than the raw comparison. This becomes a proof point in D&B's sales narrative: not 'our users have lower DSO' but 'our alert causally reduces DSO by 4.2 days.'
