🧠 Data Science Topic of the Day

🎓 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

Teaching models to align with human judgment — the engine behind every useful LLM you've deployed.

What It Is

RLHF is a training technique that fine-tunes a language or decision model using feedback signals derived from human preferences rather than fixed labels. Instead of telling a model 'the correct answer is X,' you show human raters pairs of outputs and ask which is better. Those preferences train a separate Reward Model, which then guides the main model via reinforcement learning (typically Proximate Policy Optimization, PPO) to produce outputs humans actually prefer. It's why ChatGPT gives structured, helpful responses instead of statistically likely but useless ones. RLHF sits at the intersection of supervised learning, reward modeling, and RL — and is the dominant technique behind aligning large language models (LLMs) to real-world task quality.

How It Works

Step 1 — Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Start with a pretrained base LLM and fine-tune it on a curated dataset of high-quality prompt-response pairs. This gives the model a reasonable starting policy. Step 2 — Reward Model Training: Present human raters with multiple model outputs for the same prompt. Raters rank them by quality (accuracy, helpfulness, safety). These rankings become training data for a separate Reward Model (RM) — typically another LLM with a scalar output head — that learns to predict human preference scores for any given output. Step 3 — RL Fine-Tuning via PPO: The SFT model becomes the 'policy.' It generates outputs; the Reward Model scores them. PPO updates the policy to maximize reward scores while a KL-divergence penalty prevents the model from drifting too far from the original SFT baseline (avoiding reward hacking — gaming the RM with nonsensical but high-scoring outputs). Step 4 — Iteration: The cycle repeats with fresh human feedback on the updated model. A modern variant, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), skips the explicit RM entirely and optimizes preferences directly into the policy, reducing complexity and instability. Key hyperparameters to control: KL penalty coefficient (β), PPO clip ratio, reward model capacity vs. policy capacity, and human rater consistency (inter-annotator agreement).

When To Use

1. You have a generative model producing outputs that are technically fluent but misaligned with business quality standards — e.g., hallucinated company names, wrong firmographic data, off-tone sales copy. 2. You cannot enumerate all correct answers with a labeled dataset but humans can reliably rank outputs. 3. You need to encode nuanced, subjective criteria (e.g., 'a good account summary is concise AND actionable') that resist simple loss functions. 4. You're deploying an LLM in a high-stakes workflow (credit risk commentary, compliance flags) where output tone and factual grounding matter enormously. Avoid RLHF when: your raters are inconsistent or low-quality (garbage reward model = garbage policy), your budget for human annotation is negligible, or a simpler prompt-engineering or DPO approach achieves the same alignment at lower cost.

RLHF fix: (1) SFT phase — fine-tune on 5,000 hand-curated 'gold standard' account briefs written by top sales analysts. (2) Reward Model — present 200 D&B sales reps with pairs of AI-generated summaries for 500 real accounts; reps pick the one they'd actually act on. Train a Reward Model on these 100,000 pairwise comparisons. The RM learns to score summaries higher when they: lead with a Paydex risk flag if score < 60, cite a specific buying signal (e.g., recent headcount growth in IT), keep total length under 120 words, and name the correct ultimate parent entity. (3) PPO loop — run 3 rounds of RL fine-tuning, each time generating new summaries, scoring with the RM, and updating the policy. KL penalty set high enough to prevent the model from learning to mention 'Paydex' robotically in every summary regardless of relevance. (4) Result — in a held-out evaluation, rep-preferred rate for AI summaries rises from 41% (SFT only) to 79% (post-RLHF).
