🧠 Data Science Topic of the Day

🎓 A/B Testing & Experimentation Frameworks

Replacing gut-feel decisions with causal evidence — at scale, in production.

What It Is

A/B testing is the discipline of randomly assigning users, accounts, or model variants to two or more treatment conditions and measuring the causal effect of that change on a target metric. It is the gold standard for validating product, model, and go-to-market decisions because randomisation breaks confounding — you know the difference in outcomes is caused by the treatment, not by who happened to see it. Modern experimentation frameworks extend classical two-sample t-tests into sequential testing, multi-armed bandits, switchback designs for network effects, and CUPED variance reduction, making experiments faster and cheaper without inflating false-positive rates.

How It Works

1. HYPOTHESIS & UNIT OF RANDOMISATION — Define what you are changing (a model score threshold, a UI element, an email subject line) and the unit you randomise over (user, account, DUNS number, territory). The unit must be the same as the unit of interference — if accounts talk to each other (network effects), randomising by account can leak treatment into control. 2. SAMPLE SIZE & POWER CALCULATION — Before running, compute required N using: n = 2σ²(z_α + z_β)² / δ², where δ is the minimum detectable effect (MDE), σ² is outcome variance, z_α is the critical value for your false-positive rate (typically 0.05), and z_β for power (typically 0.80). Undersized experiments produce noisy results; oversized ones waste time. 3. RANDOMISATION & ASSIGNMENT — Use a deterministic hash (e.g. SHA-256(entity_id + experiment_id) mod 100) so assignment is stable across sessions but unpredictable. Stratified or blocked randomisation on key covariates (industry, firmographic segment, historical spend tier) reduces variance without bias. 4. GUARDRAIL & PRIMARY METRICS — Choose ONE primary metric (e.g. pipeline conversion rate) and several guardrail metrics (e.g. data quality score, churn rate) that must not degrade. Testing multiple primary metrics inflates Type I error — use Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg correction if unavoidable. 5. CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) — A powerful variance reduction technique. Regress the outcome Y on a pre-experiment covariate X (same metric measured before the experiment): Y_cuped = Y - θ(X - E[X]), where θ = Cov(Y,X)/Var(X). This can cut required sample sizes by 30–70% with zero bias cost. 6. SEQUENTIAL TESTING & EARLY STOPPING — Classical fixed-horizon tests are invalidated by peeking. Sequential tests (e.g. always-valid p-values using e-values, or alpha-spending functions like O'Brien-Fleming) let you monitor continuously and stop early for efficacy or futility without inflating false-positive rates. 7. STATISTICAL INFERENCE — Compute the test statistic, confidence interval, and p-value. For conversion metrics use a z-test on proportions; for revenue or continuous metrics use Welch's t-test or bootstrap CIs if distributions are heavy-tailed. Report effect size with CI, not just p-value significance. 8. SHIP/NO-SHIP DECISION — Combine statistical significance with practical significance (is δ large enough to matter commercially?) and guardrail status. Log everything to an experiment registry (e.g. integrated with MLflow or a dedicated platform like Statsig, Eppo, or GrowthBook).

When To Use

Use A/B testing whenever you need causal evidence rather than correlation — launching a new predictive model score into a sales workflow (does it actually lift pipeline?), testing email personalisation driven by intent signals, validating a new territory carving algorithm, or comparing two entity resolution strategies on match precision. Do NOT use it when: (a) you cannot randomise (e.g. one market, one product launch), use difference-in-differences or synthetic control instead; (b) the effect takes years to materialise; (c) ethical constraints prevent withholding a clearly superior treatment. Prefer multi-armed bandits over pure A/B when you want to minimise regret (route traffic to the winner dynamically) — useful for real-time model serving decisions.

🎯 Worked Example — D&B Context

SCENARIO — D&B's Prospecting Agent surfaces a ranked list of target accounts to an inside sales rep each morning. The data science team has trained a new Buydex-enhanced propensity model (v2) and wants to know if it actually increases qualified meetings booked versus the current Paydex-only model (v1). STEP 1 — Unit: Randomise at the DUNS entity level (not rep level) to avoid rep-level behavioural confounding. Stratify by industry vertical and company size decile. STEP 2 — Metrics: Primary = qualified meetings booked per account surfaced (7-day window). Guardrails = data quality complaint rate, rep satisfaction score, pipeline attribution accuracy. STEP 3 — Sample size: Historical conversion rate = 4.2%. MDE = 0.6pp (a meaningful lift). At α=0.05, power=0.80: n ≈ 17,400 accounts per arm. With ~40K accounts active in the prospecting pool, a two-week run is sufficient. STEP 4 — CUPED: Use each account's trailing 90-day meeting booking rate as the pre-experiment covariate. This reduces variance by ~45%, cutting the required run time nearly in half. STEP 5 — Sequential monitoring: Use an O'Brien-Fleming alpha-spending function so the team can check results weekly without p-hacking. Set a futility boundary at week 1 — if the observed effect is less than 20% of MDE, stop early and avoid wasting rep time. STEP 6 — Result: After 10 days, v2 shows +0.8pp lift (CI: [0.3pp, 1.3pp], p=0.003). Guardrails all green. Ship decision: roll v2 to 100% of the prospecting pool. STEP 7 — Log to experiment registry with full lineage: model version, feature set, assignment hash seed, CUPED covariate, result, and ship rationale. This becomes ground-truth evidence for the next model iteration and for explaining to sales leadership why the AI recommendation changed.
