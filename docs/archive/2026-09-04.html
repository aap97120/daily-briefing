🧠 Data Science Topic of the Day

🎓 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

How you teach a model not just to be accurate, but to be useful — by learning from human judgment.

What It Is

RLHF is the training technique behind most modern large language models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini). It fine-tunes a base model using human preference signals rather than fixed labels. Instead of telling the model 'this answer is correct,' you show it two outputs and have a human say 'this one is better.' Over thousands of such comparisons, the model learns what 'good' looks like according to actual human judgment. The result is a model that doesn't just predict tokens statistically — it produces outputs aligned with what people actually find helpful, accurate, and appropriate. For D&B, RLHF is the mechanism that would make any fine-tuned D&B agent genuinely useful in practice — because 'correct' in B2B sales contexts isn't just factually accurate, it's commercially actionable.

How It Works

RLHF has three distinct stages. Stage 1 — Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Start with a pre-trained base LLM and fine-tune it on a curated dataset of high-quality prompt/response pairs. This gets the model in the right neighborhood. Stage 2 — Reward Model Training: Collect human preference data by showing annotators pairs of model outputs for the same prompt and asking them to rank which is better. Train a separate 'reward model' (RM) — a classifier that learns to score any output on a scale from bad to good, based on those human preferences. Stage 3 — PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization): Use reinforcement learning to update the main model. The model generates an output, the reward model scores it, and that score becomes the training signal. PPO is the RL algorithm that updates model weights while preventing it from drifting too far from the original SFT model in one step (the 'proximal' constraint). A KL-divergence penalty term keeps the model from 'reward hacking' — exploiting loopholes in the reward model by producing outputs that score high but are actually nonsensical. A newer, simpler alternative is DPO (Direct Preference Optimization), which skips the separate reward model entirely and encodes preferences directly into the loss function, making it cheaper to train and less prone to instability.

When To Use

Use RLHF (or DPO) when: (1) You have a fine-tuned model that produces technically correct outputs but misses the mark on tone, format, or commercial relevance — it's accurate but not useful. (2) 'Correct' is subjective or context-dependent, like ranking account prioritization rationale or drafting outreach emails. (3) You need to suppress specific failure modes — hallucinated financials, over-confident risk scores, legally sensitive language. (4) You're building an internal copilot or agent and want it to match how your best analysts actually think and communicate. Do NOT use it when you have fewer than a few hundred preference pairs — the reward model won't generalize. DPO is often the better practical choice for enterprise teams without RL infrastructure.

🎯 Worked Example — D&B Context

D&B's Prospecting Agent ingests 600M+ business records and generates account summaries with recommended outreach angles. After initial SFT fine-tuning on historical analyst notes, the outputs are technically grounded but commercially flat — they describe a company accurately but don't surface the buying signal. D&B runs an RLHF loop: senior sales analysts review pairs of generated account briefs and mark which one they'd actually act on. One brief might cite a Paydex score drop and note 'credit risk elevated'; the preferred brief says 'Paydex dropped 18 points in 90 days following a CFO change — classic tightening cycle, call the VP Finance now, not the procurement team.' The reward model learns that specificity + urgency + stakeholder routing = high preference score. After PPO fine-tuning, the agent consistently produces briefs that senior reps rate as 'ready to use' rather than 'needs editing.' Crucially, the reward model also learns to penalize hallucinated revenue figures — a critical guardrail when D&B's verified financials are a core trust asset. This is D&B's 'Explainability First' principle operationalized: the model doesn't just output a score, it outputs a reasoning chain that mirrors how a human expert would present it.
