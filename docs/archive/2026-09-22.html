🧠 Data Science Topic of the Day

🎓 Survival Analysis

Predicting not just IF something will happen — but WHEN.

What It Is

Survival analysis is a family of statistical methods originally developed in medical research to model the time until an event occurs — a patient dying, a machine failing, a customer churning. In a B2B context, that 'event' could be a business going delinquent on payments, a customer churning, a company filing for bankruptcy, or a deal closing. Unlike standard classification (which outputs a binary yes/no), survival analysis outputs a probability curve over time — telling you the likelihood an entity survives past day 30, day 90, day 180, and so on. The key insight: time itself is a feature, and censored data (observations where the event hasn't happened yet) is information, not missing data. The two workhorses are the Kaplan-Meier estimator (non-parametric, great for visualization) and the Cox Proportional Hazards model (semi-parametric, great for understanding which covariates accelerate or delay the event).

How It Works

1. DEFINE THE EVENT & TIMELINE: Clearly specify what constitutes the event (e.g., first payment delinquency) and the time origin (e.g., date of first trade line). 2. HANDLE CENSORING: Observations where the event hasn't occurred yet (customer still active, company still solvent) are 'right-censored' — they contribute partial information. Dropping them would bias results; survival analysis uses them correctly. 3. KAPLAN-MEIER CURVES: Estimate the survival function S(t) = P(T > t) non-parametrically. At each observed event time, the curve steps down. Comparing curves across segments (e.g., industry verticals) reveals which cohorts deteriorate fastest. 4. COX PROPORTIONAL HAZARDS: Models the hazard rate h(t) = h₀(t) × exp(β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + ...). The baseline hazard h₀(t) is left unspecified (semi-parametric). Coefficients β tell you the multiplicative effect of each covariate on risk — a Paydex score drop of 10 points might multiply delinquency hazard by 1.4x. 5. MODERN EXTENSIONS: DeepSurv (neural network Cox model), Random Survival Forests (handles non-linear interactions, no proportionality assumption), and competing risks models (e.g., a business can churn OR go bankrupt — each is a competing event). 6. OUTPUT: A survival curve per entity, a median time-to-event estimate, and hazard ratios for each predictor.

When To Use

Use survival analysis when the TIMING of an outcome matters as much as the outcome itself. Classic triggers: (a) Customer churn modeling — not just who will churn but when, enabling tiered intervention urgency. (b) Credit risk — predicting time to first delinquency or default, not just probability of default. (c) Pipeline risk — which deals will go dark and how soon, so reps prioritize the right opportunities. (d) Supplier risk — which vendors will fail within 90 days vs. 12 months. (e) Upsell/expansion timing — modeling time to expansion event to identify the optimal outreach window. Do NOT use it when you only care about a binary outcome with no time dimension, or when you have very short, uniform observation windows.

🎯 Worked Example — D&B Context

D&B's Paydex scores, trade payment histories, and corporate family tree data feed a Random Survival Forest model predicting time-to-delinquency for 50M+ business entities. Here's the mechanics: Event = first payment 30+ days past due. Time origin = date of most recent Paydex score refresh. Covariates = current Paydex score, 12-month Paydex trajectory (slope), industry vertical, company age, employee count band, number of active trade lines, parent company health score, and macroeconomic sector index. The model outputs, per DUNS number, a survival curve: 'This entity has an 82% probability of remaining current through 90 days, dropping to 61% at 180 days.' For D&B's AI Decisioning layer embedded in Salesforce Agentforce, the Pipeline Risk Agent surfaces not just 'this account is at risk' but 'based on payment behavior trajectory, this account has a median time-to-delinquency of 67 days — act within 30 days to renegotiate terms.'
