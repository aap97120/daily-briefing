🧠 Data Science Topic of the Day

🎓 Knowledge Graphs

Connecting the dots between entities so machines can reason about relationships, not just records.

What It Is

A knowledge graph is a structured network where nodes represent real-world entities (companies, people, products, locations) and edges represent typed, directional relationships between them (owns, supplies, competes with, employs). Unlike a flat table or a document store, a knowledge graph captures context — the meaning of how things relate. Each relationship is a first-class fact, not just a foreign key. The result is a queryable, traversable map of the world that can power reasoning, discovery, and inference. At D&B, this is essentially what the corporate family tree and DUNS entity resolution network already are: a global knowledge graph of 600M+ business entities with typed relationships (subsidiary of, headquartered in, cross-sells with, pays via).

How It Works

Knowledge graphs are built on three core primitives: (1) Triples — every fact is a (Subject, Predicate, Object) statement, e.g. (Acme Corp, subsidiary_of, GlobalCo). These map to the RDF/OWL standard or property graph formats like those used by Neo4j. (2) Ontologies — a schema layer that defines what entity types exist and what relationships are valid between them, enforcing logical consistency. (3) Graph traversal & inference — queries written in SPARQL or Cypher walk multi-hop paths ('find all tier-2 suppliers of companies in my portfolio that are also flagged as high payment risk') that would require many expensive joins in SQL. Graph neural networks (GNNs) extend this further: they learn vector embeddings for each node by aggregating features from its neighbors iteratively, letting ML models exploit structural position in the graph, not just node attributes. Link prediction is a key task — inferring missing edges (e.g. 'this company is likely also a customer of that vendor') from patterns in the existing graph. Knowledge graphs also integrate with LLMs via graph-RAG: the LLM retrieves subgraphs as grounded context before generating an answer, solving the hallucination problem by anchoring responses to verified relational facts.

When To Use

Use a knowledge graph when: (1) your core analytical problem is relational — you need to traverse chains of ownership, influence, or risk rather than score individual rows; (2) entity resolution across heterogeneous data sources is critical (same company, multiple name spellings, multiple IDs); (3) you need explainable multi-hop reasoning ('why is this account flagged?') rather than a black-box score; (4) your domain has a rich, stable ontology (B2B commerce, healthcare, legal, financial services); (5) you want to augment an LLM agent with structured world knowledge without hallucination. Avoid when data is sparse and relationships are weak, when you have no team expertise in graph tooling, or when a simple relational schema genuinely covers your use case.

🎯 Worked Example — D&B Context

D&B's Prospecting Agent needs to surface 'companies likely to be in-market for supply chain finance products.' A flat propensity model scores each company on its own attributes. A knowledge graph goes further: it traverses the corporate family tree to find that TargetCo is a subsidiary of a parent with deteriorating Paydex scores, is itself a tier-1 supplier to three companies in a flagging industry vertical, and shares two board members with a company that recently took on a supply chain finance product. Each hop is a logical, auditable reasoning step — not a black-box weight. The graph query: MATCH (t:Company {id:'TargetCo'})-[:SUBSIDIARY_OF]->(p:Company)-[:PAYDEX_TREND]->(trend {direction:'declining'}) RETURN p, trend. This surfaces risk context invisible to any row-level model. Layered with GNN embeddings, D&B can also perform link prediction — inferring probable buyer-supplier relationships that haven't been explicitly recorded, expanding the Buydex signal. When packaged as a graph-RAG tool inside Microsoft Copilot or Salesforce Agentforce, the agent can answer 'what is the ownership and payment risk profile of this prospect's entire corporate family?' in natural language, with every claim traceable to a specific graph edge — pure Explainability First in practice.
